# Energy Derivatives Valuation Toolkit

[![CI](https://github.com/rxj0102/energy-derivatives-valuation-toolkit/actions/workflows/ci.yml/badge.svg)](https://github.com/rxj0102/energy-derivatives-valuation-toolkit/actions/workflows/ci.yml)

A small, self-contained Python toolkit that simulates wholesale electricity
and natural gas prices and uses that simulation to price energy derivatives
and value two common power-market assets: a gas-fired "tolling agreement"
and a battery storage system. It ends with a client-style portfolio risk
report (VaR / CVaR).

It was built as a demonstration of quantitative energy-market modeling —
the kind of work an Energy Analyst at a firm like cQuant or Zema Global
does: building spot price models, pricing spread options, valuing
generation and storage assets as real options, and reporting portfolio
risk.

> **⚠️ DISCLAIMER: All data in this repository is synthetic.** Prices are
> generated by a stochastic model with parameters chosen to be
> *qualitatively* realistic (seasonality, spikes, a plausible power/gas
> relationship) — none of it is calibrated to a real ISO, hub, or vendor
> feed. **Nothing here should be used for trading, hedging, or any real
> financial decision.** It exists to demonstrate modeling technique.

## Why this exists, in plain language

Wholesale electricity is unlike almost any other traded commodity: it
can't be stored economically at scale, demand swings hard with weather,
and the whole market is built on physical constraints (power plants,
transmission lines, ramp rates). That combination produces price behavior
you don't see in stocks or bonds:

- **Strong seasonality.** Prices are higher in summer (air conditioning
  load) and, to a lesser extent, winter (heating load).
- **Mean reversion.** A price spike today doesn't mean prices stay high —
  once the heat wave or outage passes, prices snap back toward a "normal"
  level within days.
- **Spikes.** Because supply is physically constrained, a hot afternoon or
  a generator outage can send prices up 5-50x for a few hours or days,
  then they fall back. This is very different from the smooth, roughly
  lognormal behavior assumed by textbook option pricing models.

This toolkit builds a price model that reproduces those features, then
prices financial instruments and physical assets on top of it.

### Key concepts, explained

**Spark spread.** The profit margin of a gas-fired power plant: the price
it sells power for, minus the cost of the gas it burns to make that power.
Burning gas isn't perfectly efficient — a plant's *heat rate* (measured in
MMBtu of gas per MWh of electricity produced) tells you how much fuel it
takes. So:

```
spark spread ($/MWh) = power price − heat_rate × gas price − variable O&M cost
```

If the spread is positive, running the plant makes money; if it's
negative, the plant should sit idle. This spread is the fundamental
economic driver behind gas plant valuation, and it behaves like the payoff
of a financial option — you only "exercise" (run the plant) when it's
profitable.

**Tolling agreement.** A financial contract where the holder pays for the
right to run a power plant (converting gas into power) without owning it
— they pay the plant's operating costs and keep the spark-spread profit
each time they choose to run it. Economically, this is a strip of daily
spark-spread call options, complicated by real operating constraints: you
can only start the plant a limited number of times per month, and once
started it has to run for some minimum stretch of time. Valuing a tolling
agreement means solving, along many simulated price paths, when the
optimal times to run the plant are, given those constraints — that's a
*real options* valuation problem.

**Swing / storage option.** A battery doesn't just take a single yes/no
decision — it can charge or discharge at (almost) any time, subject to its
power rating (how fast it can charge/discharge) and energy capacity (how
much it can hold), with some energy lost to inefficiency on every round
trip. The right decision today depends on how full the battery already is
*and* on the price. This family of multi-exercise, state-dependent options
is called a "swing option," and it needs a genuinely different valuation
technique than a plain option — here, a Longstaff-Schwartz-style
Least-Squares Monte Carlo (LSM) approach.

**Forward curve.** A schedule of prices for power delivered in future
months, built either by "bootstrapping" a historical seasonal shape onto a
current price level (the classic desk-practitioner method) or by taking
the model-implied expected future price directly from the stochastic
model's equations.

**VaR / CVaR.** Value-at-Risk answers "how bad could next month look, 95%
of the time?" — it's the loss threshold that's only exceeded 5% of the
time (at 95% confidence). Conditional VaR (a.k.a. Expected Shortfall) goes
a step further and answers "given that we *are* in that bad 5% of
outcomes, how bad is it on average?" CVaR is always at least as large as
VaR and is considered the more informative, better-behaved risk measure.

## Project layout

```
energy_toolkit/
    power_price_model.py           # spot price simulator (gas + power, seasonal jump-diffusion)
    forward_curve.py                # forward curve bootstrap (historical-shape + model-implied)
    derivatives_pricing.py          # Black-76, Margrabe, Kirk approximation, Monte Carlo pricers
    generation_asset_valuation.py   # tolling agreement (gas plant) real-options valuation
    battery_storage_optionality.py  # battery storage valuation via Longstaff-Schwartz
    model_calibration_validation.py # moment-matching calibration + backtesting
    risk_report.py                  # portfolio VaR/CVaR report
tests/                               # pytest suite (44 tests)
```

### Module-by-module

**`power_price_model.py`** — the foundation everything else builds on.
Natural gas is simulated as a seasonal, mean-reverting (Ornstein-Uhlenbeck)
process on log-price. Power price is built as

```
power_price = heat_rate × gas_price + scarcity_margin
```

where `scarcity_margin` is itself a seasonal mean-reverting jump-diffusion
process (the premium the marginal generator earns above fuel cost, with a
summer-and-winter seasonal shape and a Poisson jump component whose
intensity is elevated in summer). This gives the model a built-in,
economically coherent link between power and gas — exactly what the spark
spread modules downstream need — rather than just simulating two unrelated
series and bolting on a correlation coefficient.

**`forward_curve.py`** — builds a forward price curve two ways: (1) a
classic *historical-shape bootstrap* (normalize a historical/simulated
seasonal monthly pattern and anchor it to a recent price level), and (2) a
*model-implied* curve using the closed-form/semi-analytic conditional
expectation of the mean-reverting jump-diffusion process. The two are
compared as a cross-check.

**`derivatives_pricing.py`** — forwards/swaps, European options (Black-76
closed form vs. Monte Carlo), and spread options (Margrabe closed form for
a zero-strike exchange option, Kirk's approximation for a non-zero strike,
and Monte Carlo for both, plus Monte Carlo pricing directly against the
full jump-diffusion spot model — which will *not* match Black-76, on
purpose, since that mismatch quantifies the model risk of using a vanilla
lognormal option formula on a spiky, mean-reverting underlying).

**`generation_asset_valuation.py`** — values a synthetic gas plant tolling
agreement as a strip of daily spark-spread options with a monthly
start-count limit and a minimum-run-time constraint, solved exactly via a
small dynamic program (per month, per simulated path) and averaged across
Monte Carlo paths.

**`battery_storage_optionality.py`** — values a battery via a
Longstaff-Schwartz / Least-Squares Monte Carlo approach adapted to storage
(discretized state-of-charge grid, regression-based backward induction,
forward pass with realized cash flows under the fitted policy — the
Boogert & de Jong 2008 method).

**`model_calibration_validation.py`** — calibrates a subset of the price
model's parameters to match target statistical moments (mean, volatility,
skewness, spike frequency) via `scipy.optimize`, and validates the fit
out-of-sample, plus a forward-curve-vs-realized-spot backtest.

**`risk_report.py`** — combines a tolling agreement, a battery, and a
fixed-for-floating swap hedge into one book, valued on the *same* set of
simulated price paths (so the books' outcomes are properly correlated),
and reports portfolio VaR/CVaR alongside each book's standalone risk for a
diversification-benefit comparison.

python -m venv .venv

## Running the tests

```bash
pytest
```

The suite (44 tests) covers: price process statistical properties
(seasonality, mean reversion, jump clustering, skewness), Black-76 vs.
Monte Carlo convergence and put-call parity, Margrabe/Kirk vs. Monte Carlo
convergence for spread options, dispatch-DP correctness (cross-checked
against brute force), LSM convergence for the battery, and VaR/CVaR
correctness (including a check against the analytic normal-distribution
quantile). It runs in well under a minute.

## Quick start

```python
from energy_toolkit.power_price_model import PowerMarketSimulator

sim = PowerMarketSimulator()
scenario = sim.simulate_paths(n_years=5.0, n_paths=1000, seed=42)
# scenario.power_prices, scenario.gas_prices: arrays of shape (n_paths, n_steps)

# Price a European call on power via Black-76 vs. Monte Carlo
from energy_toolkit.derivatives_pricing import black76_price, monte_carlo_black76
F, K, T, r, sigma = 55.0, 60.0, 1.0, 0.04, 0.65
print(black76_price(F, K, T, r, sigma, "call"))
print(monte_carlo_black76(F, K, T, r, sigma, n_paths=200_000, seed=1))

# Value a tolling agreement
from energy_toolkit.generation_asset_valuation import TollingAgreementSpec, value_tolling_agreement
spec = TollingAgreementSpec(capacity_mw=100, heat_rate=7.5, max_starts_per_month=8, min_run_days=1)
result = value_tolling_agreement(scenario, spec, r=0.04)
print(result["mean_value"], "+/-", result["std_error"])

# Value a battery
from energy_toolkit.battery_storage_optionality import BatterySpec, value_battery_storage
battery_spec = BatterySpec(power_mw=25, energy_capacity_mwh=100, round_trip_efficiency=0.85)
battery_result = value_battery_storage(scenario.power_prices, battery_spec, r=0.04)
print(battery_result["mean_value"])

# Portfolio risk report
from energy_toolkit.risk_report import SwapHedgeSpec, generate_risk_report, format_report_text
swap_spec = SwapHedgeSpec(fixed_price=55.0, notional_mwh_per_day=80.0, position="short_power")
report = generate_risk_report(scenario, spec, battery_spec, swap_spec, r=0.04)
print(format_report_text(report))
```

No pre-computed valuation numbers are included in this README — run the
snippets above (or the test suite) to see real output from this code on
your machine.

## Known simplifications (disclosed, not hidden)

- The forward curve and derivative prices are built under the real-world
  (physical) measure with no market price of risk / risk premium applied —
  a simplification, not generally true of real commodity markets.
- Power price is floored at a small positive value; real wholesale markets
  do occasionally see negative prices (renewable oversupply), which this
  model does not represent.
- The battery model discretizes state-of-charge onto a grid and uses a
  regression-based (not exact) dynamic program; this is a standard,
  well-precedented approximation (Boogert & de Jong, 2008) but is not a
  closed-form solution, and fitting error can accumulate over many time
  steps.
- Calibration in `model_calibration_validation.py` matches a handful of
  target statistical moments using a Monte Carlo objective evaluated on a
  modest, fixed-seed sample (for tractability/speed) — it is a
  demonstration of the calibration *procedure*, not a precision fit.
